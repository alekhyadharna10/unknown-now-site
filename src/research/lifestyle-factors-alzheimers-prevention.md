## Intro

Cognitive function, memory, and the capacity to do daily tasks all continuously deteriorate as a result of Alzheimer’s disease, a chronic neurological condition that causes brain cell loss and degeneration<sup>1</sup>. It is the most frequent cause of dementia, a collection of brain abnormalities that lead to the loss of social and intellectual abilities. Early symptoms include moderate disorientation and memory loss<sup>1</sup>. As the illness worsens, people may encounter significant memory loss, trouble speaking and interacting with others, confusion, mood fluctuations, and behavioral abnormalities. Three stages of Alzheimer’s disease commonly occur: mild (early stage), moderate (middle stage), and severe (late stage)<sup>2</sup>. Worsening symptoms and greater care requirements are hallmarks of each stage. Only after death, by examining brain tissue, can a definitive diagnosis be made. There is mounting evidence to show that certain lifestyle choices may impact an individual’s risk of Alzheimer’s disease. These elements may impact mental health and general wellbeing, possibly postponing the beginning or reducing the progression of Alzheimer’s disease<sup>3</sup>.

## Physical activity

Exercise on a regular basis has been repeatedly associated with a lower risk of Alzheimer’s disease and other types of dementia<sup>4</sup>. Physical activity can help preserve cognitive abilities and postpone the onset of Alzheimer’s disease symptoms. Enhancing cardiovascular health through exercise promotes appropriate cerebral blood flow. Sufficient blood flow guarantees that brain cells get enough oxygen and nutrients, which are essential to their survival and functionality<sup>4</sup>. Exercise assists in decreasing the overall risk of cardiovascular disease by lowering the chance of diseases like diabetes, and hypertension alike. Brain health and cardiovascular health are intimately related<sup>5</sup>. The brain’s capacity to build and rearrange synaptic connections, particularly in response to learning or following injury, is known as neuroplasticity, and physical activity helps to increase it<sup>5</sup>.

**Examples of Beneficial Exercise:**

- Walking
- Jogging
- Lifting weights
- Yoga
- Tai chi

## Diet and nutrition

A healthy diet can significantly lower the chance of developing Alzheimer’s disease. Particularly, it has been demonstrated that the DASH<sup>7</sup> and Mediterranean diets promote brain health. Fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, olive oil, and lean proteins—particularly fish—are all highlighted in the Mediterranean diet<sup>6</sup>. Antioxidants, good fats, and anti-inflammatory substances found in this diet help to prevent brain damage and support general brain health<sup>6</sup>. Conversely, the DASH diet minimizes salt, sugar, and saturated fats while emphasizing fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, and low-fat dairy<sup>7</sup>. Indirectly supporting brain health and lowering the risk of Alzheimer’s disease, the DASH diet lowers blood pressure and enhances cardiovascular health<sup>7</sup>. The antioxidants in fruits and vegetables, which are abundant in both diets, help shield brain cells from oxidative stress and damage from free radicals. They include good fats, especially the omega-3 fatty acids that are present in fish and help the construction and function of brain cells. Furthermore, since inflammation in the brain is a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease, anti-inflammatory foods in these diets help lessen brain inflammation. diets strong in folic acid, B vitamins, and vitamin E are examples of nutrient-rich diets that are vital for brain health and can retard cognitive decline. People can greatly lower their risk of Alzheimer’s disease and improve overall brain health by adopting the DASH and Mediterranean diets into their everyday routines<sup>8</sup>.

## Cognitive engagement/Social interaction

The risk of Alzheimer’s disease can be decreased and general cognitive health can be enhanced by cognitive activities and strong social bonds. Reading and learning new things are examples of mental exercises that excite the brain and improve cognitive capabilities, which help to develop cognitive resilience<sup>9</sup>. In addition, maintaining social interactions through frequent interactions with loved ones and friends guards against loneliness and depression, two conditions that increase the risk of cognitive decline. Conversations during social encounters provide chances for cognitive involvement<sup>9</sup>. Cognitive exercises combined with social activities can help people develop a holistic strategy for preserving brain function and lowering their risk of Alzheimer’s disease. This integrated method improves emotional well-being in addition to brain stimulation, which raises quality of life.

## Sleep

By lowering the risk of Alzheimer’s disease and preserving brain health, getting enough good sleep is essential for a number of vital functions. The brain performs vital processes like memory consolidation as you sleep, strengthening and integrating newly acquired knowledge from the previous day<sup>10</sup>. Furthermore, the elimination of toxins, such as beta-amyloid, a protein linked to Alzheimer’s disease, that builds up in the brain throughout the day is made possible by sleep. This nocturnal clearing aids in preventing the accumulation of amyloid plaques, which over time may damage brain function<sup>10</sup>. Sleep is also essential for neuronal regeneration and repair. In order for cognitive function to perform as best it can, it supports the upkeep and repair of neurons and their connections<sup>11</sup>. On the other hand, bad sleeping patterns, such persistent sleep deprivation or sleep disorders like apnea, can negatively impact brain function. Prolonged sleep deprivation interferes with these vital functions, resulting in: memory loss, cognitive decline, and decreased performance.<sup>11</sup> Studies have demonstrated that insufficient sleep is associated with elevated brain levels of beta-amyloid up to 5%<sup>12</sup>, a protein that may facilitate the onset and advancement of Alzheimer’s disease. Inadequate sleep can also promote inflammation in the brain and other parts of the body, which aggravates cognitive decline and raises the risk of neurodegenerative illnesses<sup>12</sup>. To support brain health and reduce the risk of Alzheimer’s, it is essential to prioritize good sleep hygiene practices. This includes maintaining a regular sleep schedule, and ensuring a comfortable sleep environment. By prioritizing quality sleep, individuals can support their brain’s vital functions, and potentially lower their risk of Alzheimer’s disease later in life.
