## Introduction

We are ever changing, growing and evolving. Likewise, the brain is never stagnant. This is in part due to its constant formation of synaptic connections. However, despite these ongoing synaptic changes, there is always a stable functioning of neuronal networks. This is partly due to the homeostatic relationship between excitation and inhibition and activity-dependent adaptations. Nonetheless, there is debate regarding their intersection during development.

This paper hopes to bring understanding to how exactly activity-dependent competition between inputs and molecular signals interact during development to shape the size, shape, and functional properties of cortical modules. More specifically, we focus on axons and their changes in response to synaptic activity, and therefore the number or properties of presynaptic terminals along the axon.

## Activity-Dependent Competition Between Inputs and Molecular Signals

Cortical modules are essentially defined as compact cortical circuits that consist of vertical, interconnected columns of neurons. They emphasize synchronization of oscillatory spindles — bursts of neural oscillatory activity generated by interplay of the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN).

An axon is essentially a nerve fiber that conducts action potentials away from the cell. Inhibitory axons are able to rapidly adjust to changes in their environment by responding to local signals from postsynaptic cells (via cell adhesion molecules) and global signals from the surrounding neuronal network. This highly dynamic nature of the inhibitory system allows it to facilitate circuit rearrangements driven by changes at excitatory synapses.

This is done in part because of the continued exchange in resources, regulated by neuronal activity. Presynaptic terminals can rapidly self-assemble along axons when triggered by factors like cell adhesion molecules. For proper brain function, changes at inhibitory and excitatory synapses must be well-coordinated, as defects in this coordination can lead to circuit defects and potentially underlie psychiatric disorders. The plasticity of inhibitory and excitatory synapses is crucial for maintaining stable neuronal circuits.

The response of an inhibitory axon depends on a combination of internal factors, like availability of synaptic proteins, and external factors, like the specific cell adhesion molecules and secreted factors present. There are several open questions about the precise molecular mechanisms regulating the formation and plasticity of inhibitory synapses along axons — unraveling these mechanisms is an important future challenge for understanding synapse formation and plasticity at inhibitory connections.

The core concept tying these results together is that inhibitory axons are not static structures. Rather, the presynaptic terminals along their length are highly dynamic and can rapidly modulate their number, size, and neurotransmitter release properties in an activity-dependent fashion by harnessing processes like vesicle/protein sharing, competition for resources, and de novo terminal assembly/disassembly. This allows inhibitory connections to be continuously adjusted to maintain circuit homeostasis and facilitate plasticity based on changes in network activity patterns.

## Conclusion

More active inputs emit punishment signals that activate elimination pathways in less active neighbors while also upregulating stabilization pathways in the winners to reinforce their connectivity. This competitive process, mediated by specific molecular signals, allows the selective refinement of synapses during development.
